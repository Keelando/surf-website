How to Read Marine Data

A plain-language guide to the numbers and abbreviations used on this site.

1. Wind Speed Units: Knots, km/h, and mph

Wind speed is often measured in knots by marine services. One knot is one nautical mile per hour.

This site displays wind speeds in knots and wave heights in metres, matching the raw buoy data from Environment Canada and NOAA.
Knots (kt)km/hmphFeels like

Quick mental conversion: 1 kt ≈ 1.85 km/h ≈ 1.15 mph. To convert knots to km/h, multiply by ~1.85 (or roughly double it for a quick estimate).

2. Wind and Swell Directions

Wind direction is reported as the direction the wind is blowing from. A "NW wind" means the wind is coming from the northwest and blowing toward the southeast.

Swell (wave) direction follows the same convention on this site: the reported direction is where the waves are coming from.

A NW swell on the Strait of Georgia means waves are arriving from the northwest — they were generated further up the strait and are moving southeast toward the Fraser delta.

3. Wave Height and Sea State

Wave heights here are significant wave height (Hs) — the average height of the highest one-third of waves. Individual waves can be significantly larger.

Hs (metres)Sea stateWhat it feels like
0 – 0.1 mGlassy / calmMirror-flat water, no swell
0.1 – 0.5 mRippled / slightSmall wavelets, pleasant for kayaking and paddleboarding
0.5 – 1.0 mSlightSmall waves, occasional whitecaps; fine for most vessels
1.0 – 2.0 mModerateNoticeable rolling motion; waves may break on sandy beaches
2.0 – 4.0 mRoughLarge waves, spray; small craft should use caution
4.0 – 6.0 mVery roughVery large waves; challenging for all but large vessels
> 6.0 mHigh / phenomenalExtreme open-ocean conditions; rarely seen in the Salish Sea

The Strait of Georgia is a semi-enclosed body of water, so wave heights rarely exceed 2–3 m even in strong storms. Juan de Fuca and offshore buoys regularly see larger Pacific groundswell.

4. Reading the Data Cards

Each buoy card shows the latest observation from that station. Here's what the labels mean:

LabelWhat it means
Hs Significant wave height — average of the highest third of waves, in metres. The most-used single measure of sea state.
Tp / Peak period Peak wave period — the dominant period (in seconds) of the wave energy arriving at the buoy. Longer period (10–15 s+) = smooth groundswell from distant storms. Shorter period (3–6 s) = local chop driven by nearby wind. Longer periods mean waves carry more energy and feel more powerful even if they are not very tall.
Dominant dir. Direction the dominant swell is coming from, in compass degrees or cardinal abbreviation.
Wind speed Sustained wind speed at the buoy, in knots.
Wind gust Highest wind speed recorded during the observation window. Gusts can be 30–50% stronger than sustained wind.
Wind dir. Direction the wind is coming from.
Air temp Air temperature at the buoy, in °C.
Water temp Sea surface temperature, in °C. Useful for wetsuit planning and hypothermia risk assessment.
Pressure Atmospheric (barometric) pressure in hPa (same as millibar). Average sea-level pressure is ~1013 hPa. Falling pressure often signals approaching storms or unsettled weather.
Updated Time of the most recent observation in Pacific time. Buoys typically report every hour.
